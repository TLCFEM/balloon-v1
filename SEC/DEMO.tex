\section{Numerical Examples}\label{sec:example}
We present a few numerical examples to demonstrate the capabilities of the proposed model.
For brevity, we mostly use normalised quantities in the following examples.
In specific, stresses are normalised by the initial yield stress, which is often the initial size of the conventional yield surface; strains are normalised by the initial yield strain, which is the initial yield stress divided by the elastic modulus.
By such, the conventional yield point is always located at $(1,1)$.

For $z_r$, the average of the most recent a few (\num{2} to \num{10}) $z_r^j$ values is used unless otherwise specified.
This choice appears to be random and ad hoc, but it works well in the numerical examples presented here.

It's important to highlight that there is no goal to accurately adjust the model to match the experimental data perfectly in the following examples.
Rather, the emphasis is on showcasing the adaptability of the suggested model in qualitatively representing different cyclic behaviour.
\subsection{Stiffness Degradation}
\figref{fig:ex1} demonstrates the gradual degradation of stiffness controlled by $u$ \add{during loading phases where $z$ increases}.
The following parameters/functions are used.
\begin{gather}
    F=F_m=1,\qquad
    u=100\exp{\left(-0.2q_m\right)}.
\end{gather}
Since $u$ effectively controls how fast $z$ increases to unity, a smaller $u$ leads to a slower transition.
The proposal \secref{sec:obj_trans} does not rely on additional history variable, and implementing such a degradation is straightforward.
Alternatively, it is also possible to associate $u$ with $q$, in which case such a degradation would occur under cyclic loading as well.
\begin{figure}[ht!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics{PIC/ex1.reduction}
    \caption{stiffness degradation controlled by $u$}\label{fig:ex1}
\end{figure}
In other models, it is typically achieved by introducing a reduction factor for elastic modulus, or directly turning elastic modulus itself into a history variable \citep[e.g.,][]{Zheng2026}.

\add{It is also worth noting that $H_d$ is defaulted to zero in this example to highlight the difference between this proposal and other models, hence unloading phrases where $z$ decreases are similar to conventional models and exhibit a constant stiffness, viz., the initial elastic modulus.
    For real--world implementations, setting $H_d$ close to $F$ effectively moves the similarity center toward the boundary, thereby reducing the visual prominence of unloading regions.}
\subsection{Isotropic Hardening Stagnation}
In the example shown in \figref{fig:ex2}, isotropic hardening is fully deactivated during cyclic loading.
As a result, hysteresis loops stabilise immediately.
The following configurations are used,
\begin{gather}
    k_r=100,\qquad
    F=F_m=1+0.05q_m.
\end{gather}
In terms of $z_r$, the most recent $z_r^j$ value at load reversal is used.

\subsection{Cyclic Hardening and Softening}
The $F_c$ component is dedicated to modelling cyclic hardening/softening.
\figref{fig:cyclic_hardening} shows an example of cyclic hardening using the following expression for $H_c$.
\begin{gather}
    H_c=0.05q_m.
\end{gather}
Since $F_c$ does not evolve when $z<z_r$, the very first cycle thus sets an anchor value.
Subsequent loops would mostly contribute to $q_c$ that gradually saturates $F_c$ to $H_c$.
This process repeats whenever a new maximum $q_m$ is reached and the hysteresis loops stabilise eventually.

Noting that there is no restriction on how $H_c$ evolves, if $H_c$ decreases with $q_m$, cyclic softening can be observed as shown in
\figref{fig:cyclic_softening}, in which the following expression is used.
\begin{gather}
    H_c=-0.05q_m.
\end{gather}
However, for softening, $z_r$ may stay unchanged after reaching unity, making subsequent softening difficult if not impossible to achieve since \eqsref{eq:km} only accounts for the `relative' load level instead of the absolute one.
If $z_r$ has a short memory, e.g., depends on a limited number of recent $z_r^j$ values, it is possible to re-trigger softening by cycling at a relatively lower load level before returning to a higher load level.
However, whether such a behaviour is physically meaningful remains to be investigated.
It is also possible to refine the definitions of $K_m$ and $K_c$ to account for additional factors, but this is not pursued here for brevity.

\begin{table}[ht!]

\caption{model parameters/expressions for CS1018}\label{tab:cs1018}
\begin{tabular}{ll}
    \toprule
    evolution of $z$          & $u=400+3600\exp{\left(-1000q_m\right)}$                      \\
    isotropic hardening $F_m$ & $F_m=0.6+q_m$ (\si{\giga\pascal})                            \\
    isotropic hardening $F_c$ & $F_c=-0.1+0.1\exp{\left(-100q_m\right)}$ (\si{\giga\pascal}) \\

\end{tabular}
\end{table}
The experimental results \citep{Hassan1994} show a significant correlation between the centre of stabilised hysteresis loops and plastic history for CS1018 material.
Thus, in this example, the kinematic hardening rule adopts a combination of linear (Prager type) component and exponential (Armstrong--Frederick type) saturation component.
By choosing a negative $F_c$, cycling within a fixed strain range leads to cyclic softening.

\subsection{Q345}
The Q345 steel \citep[see][]{Xie2021a,Zheng2026} exhibits little to no cyclic hardening/softening.
Hysteresis loops stabilise immediately.
One can simply set $F_c=0$.
Calibration is relatively straightforward.
The parameters/expressions used in this example are summarised in \tabref{tab:q345}.
\begin{table}[ht!]
    \footnotesize\centering
    \caption{model parameters/expressions for Q345}\label{tab:q345}
    \begin{tabular}{ll}
        \toprule
        evolution of $z$          & $u=100+3900\exp{\left(-1000q_m\right)}$                 \\
        isotropic hardening $F_m$ & $F_m=0.33+q_m$ (\si{\giga\pascal})                     \\
        isotropic hardening $F_c$ & $F_c=0$ (\si{\giga\pascal})                             \\
        kinematic hardening       & linear (Prager type) + exponential (AF type) saturation \\
        similarity                & $H_d=0.8F$                                              \\\bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
Instead of using the mean value of recent a few $z$ values at load reversals, in this example, the maximum is used.
