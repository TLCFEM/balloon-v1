\section{Towards A Better One}
\subsection{History of Load Reversals}
In conventional plasticity models, local reversals cannot be easily detected.
A common criterion for detecting reversal is the double contraction $\beeta:\dot{\bs}$.
For example, in the multi-surface theory \citep{Semenov2022}, the exact criterion is used to identify the initiation of a load reversal, which further controls the activation of a new surface.

The normal yield ratio $z$ effectively represents the current `loading level' at any given moment.
By definition, this loading level is normalized between \num{0} and \num{1} thus does not depend on the current size of the subloading surface.
When it increases, the subloading surface expands, indicating a loading process.
When it decreases, the subloading surface contracts, indicating an unloading process.
Thus, with the introduction of $z$, local reversals can be detected with ease by checking whether $z$ ever decreases during the given (sub)step.

For a material (point) that has experienced up to $N$ local reversals, let the normal yield ratio at each load reversal be denoted as $z_r^j$ with $j=1,2,3,\cdots,N$ standing for the $j$-th reversal.
The initial value is $z_r^0=0$.
We use $z_r$ to denote some measure of the collection of all $z_r^j$, thus,
\begin{gather}
    z_r=P\left(\left\{z_r^j\right\}_{j=0}^N\right)=P\left(z_r^0,z_r^1,z_r^2,\cdots,z_r^N\right).
\end{gather}
In the above, $P\left(\cdot\right)$ is a function that maps the collection $\left\{z_r^j\right\}_{j=0}^N$ to a single scalar $z_r$.
Depending on the specific choice of function $P$, $z_r$ embodies different memory effects.
For example, $z_r$ can take the maximum value of the most recent $M$ values.
When $M=1$, $z_r$ is the most recent value $z_r^N$.
When $M\geqslant{}N$, $z_r$ is simply the maximum value of all $z_r^j$.
\subsection{Multiplicative Isotropic Hardening}
It is possible to split the accumulated plastic strain $q$ into two parts,
\begin{gather}
    q=q_m+q_r,
\end{gather}
with $q_m$ being the monotonic part and $q_r$ being the ratcheting part.
The rationale behind this split is simple: the original/conventional definition \eqsref{eq:q_original} only tracks the total amount of plastic deformation, although it is non-decreasing, there is no way to infer the corresponding loading process.

It is reasonable to assume that the total amount of plastic strain increment $\dot{q}$ within a given (sub)step would be distributed to two parts according to some certain rule, which may depend on $z_r$.
One can thus define
\begin{gather}\label{eq:split_q}
    \dot{q_m}=Q\dot{q},\qquad
    \dot{q_r}=\dot{q}-\dot{q_m}=\left(1-Q\right)\dot{q},
\end{gather}
where $Q=Q\left(z,z_r\right)$ is a function of both $z$ and $z_r$ (or equivalently, $\left\{z_r^j\right\}_{j=0}^N$) yields a split ratio between \num{0} and \num{1}.

Then the isotropic bound $\sigma^y$ can be accordingly defined as the sum of two parts,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:multiplicative_isotropic}
    \sigma^y=\sigma^y_m+\sigma^y_r,
\end{gather}
in which $\sigma^y_m=\sigma^y_m\left(q_m\right)$ and $\sigma^y_r=\sigma^y_r\left(q_r\right)$ are functions of $q_m$ and $q_r$, respectively.
\subsubsection{A Further Generalisation}
It is possible to further generalise the above two--component split to multiple components,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{q_j}=Q_j\dot{q}\qquad\text{with}\qquad\sum{}Q_j=1
\end{gather}
where $Q_j=Q_j\left(z,z_r\right)$ is the split ratio for the $j$-th component.
As a result,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:multiplicative_isotropic_general}
    \sigma^y=\sum\sigma^y_j\qquad\text{with}\qquad\sigma^y_j=\sigma^y_j\left(q_j\right).
\end{gather}
It must be pointed that \eqsref{eq:multiplicative_isotropic_general} is an extension of the multiplicative rule by \citet{Chaboche2008}.
When $\sigma^y_j$ are \textit{linear} functions of $q_j$, \eqsref{eq:multiplicative_isotropic_general} simplifies to the conventional additive rule, which is conceptually equivalent to the formulation proposed by \citet{Chaboche2008}.
For brevity, we will only consider the simpler case \eqsref{eq:split_q} and \eqsref{eq:multiplicative_isotropic} in this work.
