\section{Background}
The two-surface formulation, including bounding surface theory, has been applied to various materials, including metals \citep{Mahan2011}.
Despite differences in terminology, subloading surface theory closely resembles bounding surface theory: both use two evolving stress--space surfaces, with the outer bounding the inner, subject to isotropic and kinematic hardening.

Compared with bounding surface theory \citep{Mahan2011}, subloading surface theory is more straightforward and easier to implement \citep{Chang2025}.
As noted by \citet[][see \S~8.2.3]{Hashiguchi2017}, bounding surface theory cannot ensure smooth stiffness at the elastic--plastic transition because of its non-shrinking elastic region, which also prevents hysteresis under small one--sided cycles.
Subloading surface theory avoids these drawbacks.
However, just like other (conventional and non-conventional) plasticity models, it has limited capability in describing various cyclic phenomena, including, for example, stable hysteresis loops under fixed strain/stress amplitudes, cyclic hardening/softening, stress relaxation, etc.
As a result, simulating experimental results \citep[e.g.,][]{Hassan1992,Hassan1994,Hassan2008} would be challenging if not impossible without further enhancements.

Since hysteresis loops would inevitably accumulate plastic strain, unless the size of the active plasticity surface (being either the subloading surface, the conventional yield surface, or other appropriate surfaces in other theories) has saturated to a constant value, stable hysteresis loops cannot form.
However, experimental evidence \citep[see, e.g.,][]{Kang2002,Kang2006} has shown that further (isotropic) hardening is still possible after forming stable hysteresis loops under fixed strain/stress loading brackets.
\subsection{The Basic Framework}
To lay the foundation for subsequent discussions, we briefly summarise the general framework of subloading surface theory in this section, primarily following the formulation of \citet{Chang2025}, which is more concise yet mathematically equivalent to the original by \citet{Hashiguchi2023a}.
It should be noted that the notation system used in this work is \textit{not} fully compatible with that of the previous work \citep{Chang2025}.
\subsubsection{Constitutive Law}
Based on the additive decomposition of deformation, conventional elasticity applies.
That is, the stress tensor $\bsigma$ can be expressed as the double contraction of the elasticity tensor $\mb{D}_e$ and the elastic strain tensor $\bvarepsilon^e$,
\begin{gather}
    \bsigma=\mb{D}_e:\bvarepsilon^e=\mb{D}_e:\left(\bvarepsilon-\bvarepsilon^p\right),
\end{gather}
where $\bvarepsilon$ is the total strain tensor and $\bvarepsilon^p$ is the plastic strain tensor.
\subsubsection{Subloading Surface}
The subloading surface differs from the conventional yield surface by an additional scalar field $z$, referred to as the normal yield ratio in the existing literature.
It can be expressed as
\begin{gather}\label{eq:fs_original}
f_s=\norm{\beeta}-zF=\norm{\bs-H_\alpha\balpha+\left(z-1\right)H_d\bd}-zF,
\end{gather}
where $\beeta$ is the shifted stress tensor, $\bs=\bs\left(\bsigma\right)$ is some measure of the stress tensor $\bsigma$, in von Mises models, it is often taken as the deviatoric stress tensor $\bs=\dev{\bsigma}$, $\balpha$ is the back stress tensor normalised by $H_\alpha$, and $\bd$ is the similarity tensor \citep{Chang2025}.
Both $\balpha$ and $\bd$ are internal history variables that evolve in stress space, with their magnitudes bounded by unity.
Those two tensors are further scaled by the scalar fields $H_\alpha$ and $H_d$ respectively, which may also evolve with the development of plasticity.
For simplicity, $H_\alpha$ and $H_d$ can be taken as constant fractions of $F$, a scalar that characterises the size of the subloading surface and may be interpreted as the yield stress.

These two back stress like terms have different effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The first term $H_\alpha\balpha$ is identical to the conventional back stress term, both $\balpha$ and $H_\alpha$ evolve with the accumulated plastic strain.
          As commonly understood, this term contributes to kinematic hardening by shifting the center of the surface, and is primarily responsible for the Bauschinger effect.
    \item The second term $\left(1-z\right)H_d\bd$ has negligible influence during loading as $z$ approaches unity.
          However, during unloading when $z$ decreases, it further shifts the center of the subloading surface away from $H_\alpha\balpha$, thus effectively enhancing the Bauschinger effect.
          It also provides a mechanism to control the amount of plastic strain accumulation during one--sided and small loading cycles.
\end{itemize}
\subsubsection{Flow and Evolution Rules}
Assuming von Mises framework (thus $\bs=\dev{\bsigma}$) and associative plasticity, the flow rule takes the well--known form,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\bvarepsilon^p}=\gamma{}\pdfrac{f_s}{\bsigma}=\gamma{}\pdfrac{f_s}{\beeta}:\pdfrac{\beeta}{\bsigma}=\gamma\dfrac{\beeta}{\norm{\beeta}}=\gamma{}\bn,
\end{gather}
where $\gamma$ is the plastic multiplier.
The scalar fields are driven by the accumulated plastic strain $q$, whose rate form is defined as a function of the plastic strain rate $\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}$.
A common choice is to track the magnitude of $\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}$, thus,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:q_original}
    \dot{q}=\norm{\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}}=\gamma.
\end{gather}
For the von Mises yield criterion as expressed in \eqsref{eq:fs_original}, $\pdfrac{f_s}{\bsigma}$ happens to be normalised.
This is not always the case for other yield criteria, where further normalisation may be required to obtain the simplification $\dot{q}=\gamma$.\footnote{Such normalisation is advantageous for both formulation and implementation: a) the increment $\dot{q}$ does not depend on other tensors, making the computation of derivatives much simpler; and 2) the plastic multiplier $\gamma$ is bounded, since $\gamma=\norm{\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}}\leqslant\norm{\dot{\bvarepsilon}}$, which can help stabilise the numerical implementation.}
With the above, the following evolution rule is introduced for $z$,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:z_evolution}
    \dot{z}=U\dot{q},
\end{gather}
where $U=U\left(z\right)$ is a function of $z$.
Furthermore, the scalar bounds $H_\alpha=H_\alpha\left(q\right)$, $H_d=H_d\left(q\right)$ and $F=F\left(q\right)$ are defined as functions of $q$.
It is further required that $H_d<F$ under all circumstances, which ensures that the subloading surface is always inside the yield surface.
The tensor fields $\balpha$ and $\bd$ evolve according to Armstrong--Frederick type rules \citep{Frederick2007}.
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\balpha}=b_\alpha\left(\bn-\balpha\right)\gamma,\qquad
    \dot{\bd}=b_d\left(\bn-\bd\right)\gamma,
\end{gather}
where $b_\alpha$ and $b_d$ are material parameters that control the rate of evolution.
For the normalised back stress $\balpha$, alternatively, the linear variant can also be adopted, for example,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\balpha}=b_\alpha\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}=b_\alpha\bn\gamma.
\end{gather}
\section{Limitations of Existing Formulation}
The most distinctive feature of the subloading surface theory is that the normal yield ratio $z$ gradually approaches unity from zero during the loading process.
Thus, there is no elastic region during this process, as $z$ accumulates with plastic deformation since it is driven by $q$ as defined in \eqsref{eq:z_evolution}.
\eqsref{eq:z_evolution} can be rearranged and integrated to obtain the change of $q$ due to the change of $z$, say, for example, from $z=0$ to some $z=z^*<1$,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:int_z}
    \Delta{}q=\int_{0}^{z^*}\dfrac{1}{U}~\md{z}.
\end{gather}
Noting that regardless of the specific form of $U\left(z\right)$, the right-hand side is always deterministic (a constant), meaning that loading from $z=0$ to $z=z^*$ always requires the same amount of plastic strain accumulation $\Delta{}q$.
As in conventional models, stable hysteresis loops cannot form unless isotropic hardening saturates to a constant value.

To simulate various cyclic phenomena \citep[see, e.g.,][]{Hassan1992,Hassan1994}, a mechanism that conditionally activates or deactivates isotropic hardening (a process also referred to as hardening stagnation, non-hardening region) must be introduced.

\citet[][see, \S~2.4]{Yoshida2002} introduce an additional \textit{non-isotropic-hardening} surface in the stress space, such that isotropic hardening can be activated or deactivated based on the size of the back stress, and thus the kinematic hardening.
One should be aware that this approach does not work if kinematic hardening is not defined.
Since the evolution of the back stress is ultimately driven by the plastic strain, such an approach can be equivalently formulated in the strain space.
\citet{Ohno1982,Chaboche2008,Hashiguchi2023a} all adopt a strain space formulation.
Such an approach is widely used in the literature as a basic building block for more complex models \citep[see, e.g.,][]{Xu2016,Xie2021,Zheng2026}.
