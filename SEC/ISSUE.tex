\section{Introduction}
Predicting the constitutive behaviour of metals under cyclic loading poses a persistent difficulty in the field of solid mechanics.
Regardless of their crystalline structure, metals demonstrate complex mechanical behaviours under repeated loading.
These phenomena \citep{Suresh1998} include the Bauschinger effect, elastic/plastic shakedown, cyclic hardening or softening, and ratcheting.
While conventional plasticity theory \citep[see][]{Simo1998} adequately captures monotonic responses, it frequently lacks the fidelity required to predict these cyclic effects.
Consequently, classical frameworks have been extended to establish a variety of advanced plasticity models capable of addressing these limitations.

Among these advanced approaches, two--surface formulations --- specifically bounding surface theory \citep{Dafalias1975,Krieg1975} and subloading surface theory \citep{Hashiguchi1980,Hashiguchi1989,Hashiguchi2023a,Hashiguchi2024} --- have gained significant prominence.
These frameworks have been successfully applied to a wide range of materials, including metals \citep[e.g.,][]{Yoshida2002,Mahan2011}.
Despite differences in nomenclature, the two theories share a fundamental basis, that is, both utilize two evolving surfaces within stress space: an inner active surface enclosed by an outer bounding surface.
In both frameworks, the surfaces undergo isotropic and kinematic hardening, while the evolution of the inner surface is governed by its proximity to the outer boundary.
However, the bounding surface theory \citep{Dafalias1975,Dafalias1977,Mahan2011} is conceptually limited by its non-shrinking elastic domain/core, which inhibits the accurate simulation of hysteresis during small one-sided cycles and prevents the guarantee of smooth stiffness continuity at the elastic--plastic transition \citep[][see \S 8.2.3]{Hashiguchi2017}.
In contrast, the subloading surface theory successfully overcomes these issues while providing a more straightforward mathematical framework and much simplified numerical implementation \citep{Chang2025}.

Despite the benefits, its capability to describe all key cyclic phenomena is still subject to limitations shared by other conventional and non-conventional plasticity models.
For example, hysteresis loops intrinsically lead to the accumulation of plastic strain, meaning plastic shakedown (the formation of stable loops) requires that the active loading surface --- whether the classical yield surface or subloading surface --- has saturated to a fixed size.
This requirement stems from the theoretical premise that \textit{`the \textnormal{(unbounded)} isotropic hardening model suggests that the specimen will `shake down' to an elastic state'} \citep[][pg. 103]{Suresh1998}.
However, experimental observations \citep[e.g.,][]{Yoshida2002a,Kang2002,Kang2006} consistently show that continued cyclic hardening or softening can occur even after hysteresis loops have stabilized under fixed stress/strain amplitude cycling.
Consequently, reproducing the full range of experimental results \citep[e.g.,][]{Hassan1992,Hassan1994,Hassan2008} proves extremely challenging, if not impossible, without additional model refinements.

To simulate various cyclic phenomena, a mechanism that conditionally activates or deactivates isotropic hardening (a process also referred to as hardening stagnation, non-hardening region) must be introduced.
\citet[][see, \S~2.4]{Yoshida2002} introduced an additional \textit{non-isotropic-hardening} surface in the stress space, such that isotropic hardening can be activated or deactivated based on the size of the back stress, and thus the kinematic hardening.
One should be aware that this approach does not work if kinematic hardening is not defined, although the absence of kinematic hardening is not practically useful.
Since the evolution of the back stress is ultimately driven by the plastic strain, such an approach can be equivalently formulated in the strain space.
\citet{Ohno1982,Chaboche2008,Hashiguchi2023a} all adopt a strain space formulation.
Such an approach is widely used in the literature as a basic building block for more complex models \citep[see, e.g.,][]{Xu2016,Xie2021,Zheng2026}.
Although recent efforts typically incorporate both isotropic and kinematic hardening \citep[e.g.,][]{Yang2023}, improvements in cyclic response have often relied primarily on increasingly complex kinematic hardening rules.

In response to these challenges, the present work introduces a novel and efficient constitutive framework.
This framework is achieved by incorporating a flexible isotropic hardening rule, grounded in the additive decomposition of plastic strain, with targeted enhancements to the subloading surface theory.
The resulting model demonstrates the capability to accurately reproduce a broad spectrum of complex cyclic behaviors, including stable hysteresis loops, cyclic hardening or softening, and the previously intractable phenomenon of work hardening stagnation.


The rest of this paper is organised as follows.
In \secref{sec:background}, we briefly summarise the general framework of subloading surface theory.
In \secref{sec:proposal}, the proposed enhancements to the model are presented, including a split of plastic strain based on load reversals, a refined isotropic hardening rule that accounts for contributions from different plastic strain components, and other necessary modifications that further improve the model's flexibility.
In \secref{sec:example}, we first demonstrate the model's capabilities through several simple examples, followed by a few more complex cases that simulate experimental results from the literature, demonstrating the effectiveness of the proposed model.
\section{Background}\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Basic Framework}
To lay the foundation for subsequent discussions, we briefly summarise the general framework of subloading surface theory in this section, primarily following the formulation of \citet{Chang2025}, which is more concise yet mathematically equivalent to the original by \citet{Hashiguchi2023a}.
It should be noted that the notation system used in this work is \textit{not} fully compatible with that of the previous work \citep{Chang2025}.
\subsubsection{Constitutive Law}
Based on the additive decomposition of deformation, conventional elasticity applies.
That is, the stress tensor $\bsigma$ can be expressed as the double contraction of the elasticity tensor $\mb{D}_e$ and the elastic strain tensor $\bvarepsilon^e$,
\begin{gather}
    \bsigma=\mb{D}_e:\bvarepsilon^e=\mb{D}_e:\left(\bvarepsilon-\bvarepsilon^p\right),
\end{gather}
where $\bvarepsilon$ is the total strain tensor and $\bvarepsilon^p$ is the plastic strain tensor.
\subsubsection{Subloading Surface}
The subloading surface differs from the conventional yield surface by an additional scalar field $z$, ranging from zero to unity, referred to as the normal yield ratio in the existing literature.
It can be expressed as
\begin{gather}\label{eq:fs_original}
f_s=\norm{\beeta}-zF=\norm{\bs-H_\alpha\balpha+\left(z-1\right)H_d\bd}-zF,
\end{gather}
where $\beeta$ is the shifted stress tensor, $\bs=\bs\left(\bsigma\right)$ is some measure of the stress tensor $\bsigma$, in von Mises models, it is often taken as the deviatoric stress tensor $\bs=\dev{\bsigma}$, $\balpha$ is the back stress tensor normalised by $H_\alpha$, and $\bd$ is the similarity tensor \citep{Chang2025}.
Both $\balpha$ and $\bd$ are internal history variables that evolve in the stress space, with their magnitudes bounded by unity.
Those two tensors are further scaled by the scalar fields $H_\alpha$ and $H_d$ respectively, which may also evolve with the development of plasticity.
For simplicity, $H_\alpha$ and $H_d$ can be taken as fractions of $F$, a scalar that characterises the size of the subloading surface and may be interpreted as the (reference) yield stress.

These two back stress like terms have different effects.
\begin{itemize}
    \item The first term $H_\alpha\balpha$ is identical to the conventional back stress term, both $\balpha$ and $H_\alpha$ evolve with the accumulated plastic strain.
          As commonly understood, this term contributes to kinematic hardening by shifting the center of the surface, and is primarily responsible for the Bauschinger effect.
    \item The second term $\left(1-z\right)H_d\bd$ has negligible influence during loading as $z$ approaches unity.
    However, during unloading, as $z$ decreases, the center of the active plasticity surface is further shifted away from $H_\alpha\balpha$, thereby effectively enhancing the Bauschinger effect.
    This enables the model to capture the so-called early re-yielding \citep{Yoshida2002a}.
          It also provides a mechanism to control the amount of plastic strain accumulation during one--sided and small loading cycles.
\end{itemize}

One shall note that the normal yield ratio $z$ serves a similar purpose to the variable $\delta$ in bounding surface formulations \citep{Dafalias1975,Mahan2011}.
Both variables quantify the proximity of the active plasticity surface to a reference surface, though different terminologies are used in each formulation.
\subsubsection{Flow and Evolution Rules}
Assuming von Mises framework (thus $\bs=\dev{\bsigma}$) and associative plasticity, the flow rule takes the following form,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\bvarepsilon^p}=\gamma{}\pdfrac{f_s}{\bsigma}=\gamma{}\pdfrac{f_s}{\beeta}:\pdfrac{\beeta}{\bsigma}=\gamma\dfrac{\beeta}{\norm{\beeta}}=\gamma{}\bn,
\end{gather}
where $\gamma$ is the plastic multiplier.
The scalar fields are driven by the accumulated plastic strain $q$, whose rate form is defined as a function of the plastic strain rate $\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}$.
A common choice is to track the magnitude of $\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}$, thus,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:q_original}
    \dot{q}=\norm{\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}}=\gamma.
\end{gather}
For the yield function expressed in \eqsref{eq:fs_original}, $\pdfrac{f_s}{\bsigma}$ happens to be normalised.
This is not always the case for other yield criteria, where further normalisation may be required to obtain the simplification $\dot{q}=\gamma$.\footnote{Such normalisation is advantageous for both formulation and implementation: a) the increment $\dot{q}$ does not depend on other tensors, making the computation of derivatives much simpler; and 2) the plastic multiplier $\gamma$ is bounded, since $\gamma=\norm{\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}}\leqslant\norm{\dot{\bvarepsilon}}$, which can help stabilise the numerical implementation.}
With the above, the following evolution rule is introduced for $z$,
\begin{gather}\label{eq:z_evolution}
    \dot{z}=U\dot{q},
\end{gather}
where $U=U\left(z\right)$ is a function of $z$.
Furthermore, the scalar bounds $H_\alpha=H_\alpha\left(q\right)$, $H_d=H_d\left(q\right)$ and $F=F\left(q\right)$ are defined as functions of $q$.
It is further required that $H_d<F$ under all circumstances, which ensures that the subloading surface is always inside the yield surface.
The tensor fields $\balpha$ and $\bd$ evolve according to Armstrong--Frederick type rules \citep{Frederick2007}, thus,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\balpha}=b_\alpha\left(\bn-\balpha\right)\dot{q},\qquad
    \dot{\bd}=b_d\left(\bn-\bd\right)\dot{q},
\end{gather}
where $b_\alpha$ and $b_d$ are material parameters.
For the normalised back stress $\balpha$, alternatively, the linear variant can also be adopted, for example,
\begin{gather}
    \dot{\balpha}=b_\alpha\dot{\bvarepsilon^p}=b_\alpha\bn\dot{q}.
\end{gather}
A combination of both types is also possible \citep[e.g.,][]{Yoshida2000}.
